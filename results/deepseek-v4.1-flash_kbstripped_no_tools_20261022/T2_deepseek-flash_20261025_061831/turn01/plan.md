# Plan

1. Read the raw ERSSTv5 monthly files with xarray (variable `sst`, native 2° lat/lon, surface only) and subset time to 1950-01 … 2023-12; convert longitudes to the file's own convention so the Niño 3.4 box 5°S–5°N, 170°W–120°W maps onto its grid. In parallel read the CPC index files for products `ersst5_91-20`, `ersst5_81-10` (the Niño 3.4 anomaly column of each) and `oni` (the published ONI values), aligning all four series on a common monthly time axis with pandas, and record the exact variable names/precision each file uses.

2. Build the Niño 3.4 region average of the ERSSTv5 SST field for every month: mask the field on its own 2° grid to 5°S–5°N, 170°W–120°W, then take an **area-weighted** mean over the cells inside the mask that hold valid data (weights from cos(latitude) of the native cells, restricted to valid cells), never a plain mean over lat/lon.
   - Note [omip-weighted-means-and-integrals]: on a regular grid a plain `mean()` over latitude is wrong because cells shrink toward the poles — use the area-weighted-mean operation (`compute_basic_stat`, `op='area_weighted_mean'`) with the mask applied on the field's own grid and weights restricted to valid data. The same applies to every map statistic; `compute_difference_metrics` weights by area by default and `analyze_correlation` needs `weighting='area'` — but the correlation/RMSE/offsets in steps 5, 8 and 9 are over months (one value per month), not over a map, so they stay unweighted over time.

3. From that monthly region-average SST series compute, for each calendar month separately, its climatology over the 1991-2020 period, and subtract the matching climatological month from every month of 1950-01 … 2023-12 to obtain RECON_9120 (this removes the seasonal cycle). Store the 1991-2020 climatology and `base_period='1991-2020'` in the result attributes.
   - Note [anomaly-baselines-and-climate-indices]: an anomaly must be built by removing the field's **own** monthly climatology of the chosen period (each calendar month matched to its own climatological month), and the climatology and `base_period` must be recorded in the result attrs — an anomaly is defined only relative to the period its climatology came from.

4. Repeat step 3 with the 1981-2010 monthly climatology to obtain RECON_8110 (attributes `base_period='1981-2010'`), on the same months, so the two reconstructions differ only by the baseline.

5. Compare RECON_9120 with the CPC `ersst5_91-20` Niño 3.4 anomaly over 1950-01 … 2023-12: Pearson correlation (report to 6 dp), RMSE in °C (4 dp), and the largest absolute deviation in °C (4 dp) together with the year and month in which it occurs; keep all intermediate values unrounded.

6. Compute the 3-month centred running mean of RECON_9120 (window centred on each month, so 1950-01 and 2023-12 have no running-mean value) and detect events on that running-mean series with threshold +0.5 °C and a minimum duration of 5 consecutive running-mean values; also detect La Niña events with threshold −0.5 °C and the same minimum duration for the shading in step 9. Count the El Niño events over 1950-2023 and list the three with the highest peak running-mean value, reporting for each the peak value (°C, 2 dp) and the year and month of the centre month of the peak window.
   - Note [anomaly-baselines-and-climate-indices]: events are defined operationally by persistence, not by eye — a warm/cold phase counts only if the series stays beyond the threshold for at least `min_duration`=5 consecutive steps (`detect_threshold_events`); a single step beyond the threshold is noise. State which climatology/baseline each series being compared was built on (RECON_9120 on 1991-2020, CPC `ersst5_91-20` on 1991-2020, CPC `ersst5_81-10` on 1981-2010, `oni` on its own published base period), since CPC products with different baselines are not interchangeable.

7. Apply the identical criterion of step 6 to the published CPC `oni` series directly (ONI is already the 3-month running index, so no additional smoothing): count El Niño events over its available record and report the three highest peaks with year, month and peak value (°C, 2 dp). Then report why the two counts differ, comparing the baselines the two series rest on (step 6 note), the precision at which the CPC series is distributed versus the raw ERSST-derived values, and the running-mean months lost at the ends of the record (1950-01 and 2023-12 for RECON, the ONI product's own first/last months), which truncate events that straddle a record edge.

8. Compute the reconstructed baseline offset: RECON_8110 minus RECON_9120, month by month, then the **unweighted** mean over the months 1950-01 … 2020-12 (°C, 3 dp). Do the same with the published series (CPC `ersst5_81-10` Niño 3.4 anomaly minus CPC `ersst5_91-20` Niño 3.4 anomaly) over the same months (°C, 3 dp), and quantify the agreement with the reconstructed offset (their difference, °C, at the same precision).

Top row: RECON_9120 and CPC `ersst5_91-20` monthly anomalies overlaid, the 3-month running mean of RECON_9120, the +0.5 and −0.5 °C threshold lines, and shading between the threshold and the running-mean curve — warm where a detected El Niño event (step 6) is active, cool where a detected La Niña event is active. Bottom row: RECON_9120 minus CPC on its own vertical scale. Both vertical axes labelled in °C.

Other notes:
- [anomaly-baselines-and-climate-indices] RECON_9120 and CPC `ersst5_91-20` share the 1991-2020 baseline, whereas RECON_8110 / CPC `ersst5_81-10` are on 1981-2010; two series on different baselines differ by one constant per calendar month, so the offsets in step 8 are a baseline definition, not an error.
- The offset averages in step 8 are unweighted over the months (one value per month), a different operation from the spatial area weighting used for the region mean in step 2.
- All values are carried unrounded through the steps and rounded only where reported (6 dp for r, 4 dp for RMSE/max deviation, 2 dp for peak values, 3 dp for the offsets).
