### [eof-modes-and-pdo-index] EOF modes and the PDO index: global-mean removal, area weighting, sign, explained variance and pattern amplitude
(type: method; severity: should)
#### Key points
- The PDO is "defined by the leading pattern (EOF) of sea surface temperature
  (SST) anomalies in the North Pacific basin". The anomalies are formed by
  removing both the climatological annual cycle and the global-mean SST anomaly
  at each grid point, "as a way of removing any long-term trends that might be
  associated with changes in external forcing". [CDG PDO]
- "Positive values of the PDO index correspond with negative SST anomalies in
  central and western North Pacific", with positive anomalies in the eastern
  North Pacific. [CDG PDO]
- EOFs are computed on area-weighted data; "Most commonly, these spatial
  weights are sqrt(cos(latitude))". The derived patterns "may be domain
  dependent". [CDG EOF]
#### Implications for this project
- `compute_pdo_index` removes the global-mean SST anomaly so that the index
  measures the North Pacific pattern rather than global warming, and weights
  each cell by the square root of its area so that the EOF is taken on a field
  whose variance is area-proportional.
- The sign of an EOF is arbitrary, so the tool fixes it by the convention above
  (positive phase cool in the central and western North Pacific) for both the
  pattern and the index. A negative correlation between two such patterns is
  then a real disagreement, not an arbitrary flip.
- The explained variance of a mode depends on the grid and on the length of the
  record, as well as on the domain: compare it only between fields on the same
  grid over the same period.
- The EOF itself is dimensionless and carries no amplitude. The regression
  pattern (the anomalies regressed on the standardised index, in units per
  standard deviation) is the map with a physical amplitude and the form a PDO
  pattern is normally drawn in; its area-weighted RMS is the mode's strength in
  degrees. The index is normally published standardised (zero mean, unit
  variance).
- Compare patterns from different sources on one grid with an area-weighted
  correlation (`analyze_correlation` with `weighting='area'`): the domain
  reaches high latitudes, where cells are much smaller.
#### Common mistakes
- Comparing explained variances computed on different grids or over records of
  different length.
- Reading a mode's amplitude off the EOF pattern instead of the regression
  pattern.

### [anomaly-baselines-and-climate-indices] Anomalies and climate indices: which climatology to remove, which baseline, which season convention
(type: method; severity: must)
#### Key points
- CPC changed the base period of its Oceanic Niño Index because, with a warming
  trend in the Niño-3.4 region since 1950, episodes defined against a fixed base
  period "are increasingly incorporating longer-term trends that do not reflect
  interannual ENSO variability". The ONI now uses centred 30-year base periods,
  each applied to successive 5-year spans of the record. [CPC ONI change]
  The same index therefore exists on several baselines; `configs/datasets.yaml`
  lists the local CPC products and the baseline of each.
- The WOA23 seasonal climatologies are calendar quarters: winter January–March,
  spring April–June, summer July–September, autumn October–December. [WOA23
  documentation]
#### Implications for this project
- Which climatology is removed decides what an anomaly means.
  - Against the field's **own** climatology (`compute_anomaly_vs_own_climatology`,
    and `compute_enso_nino_index` on model output) whatever bias the field
    carries cancels. This is what an index, a trend, a composite or a comparison
    between two products needs.
  - Against the **observed** climatology (`compute_anomaly_vs_woa23`) the
    field's bias against observations stays inside the anomaly. This answers
    "how far is this field from the observed ocean". An observed climatology
    forced on a model index puts the model's mean-state bias into the index
    (see `cmip6-tos-bulk-top-cell`).
- The baseline is part of the answer. An anomaly is defined only relative to
  the period its climatology came from, and two series built on different
  baselines differ by an offset (one constant per calendar month) even when
  their variability is identical. Compute an index on the baseline of any
  published series it is compared with (`base_period`), and state the
  climatology and baseline used; the tools record both in the result's attrs.
  The CPC products in the registry differ in baseline and are not
  interchangeable.
- Niño 3.4 is the standard region for ENSO monitoring. A monthly index is
  matched against the climatology of its own calendar month, which removes the
  seasonal cycle.
- Seasons: DJF/MAM/JJA/SON is the usual (meteorological) climate definition,
  while the WOA23 seasonal fields are the calendar quarters JFM/AMJ/JAS/OND.
  The two are offset by a month and must not be mixed in one comparison
  (`season_convention` of `temporal_aggregate`; `freq='season'` of
  `compute_anomaly_vs_woa23` uses the WOA23 quarters).
- Events are defined operationally by persistence, not by eye: a warm or cold
  phase of a climate index, a marine heatwave or a hypoxic spell counts only
  when the series stays beyond the threshold for a minimum number of
  consecutive steps. A single step beyond the threshold is noise;
  `min_duration` of `detect_threshold_events` is what separates an event from
  a wobble.
- Scan a time series for outliers on its anomalies, not on its raw values. A
  raw ocean series is dominated by its seasonal cycle, so every summer maximum
  sits in the tail of the distribution and the "outliers" returned are simply
  the warm season.
#### Common mistakes
- Reading the offset between a reconstructed index and a published one built on
  another baseline as an error of the reconstruction.
- Comparing seasonal means built on the DJF and the JFM conventions.

### [common-comparison-mask] One comparison sample: a shared mask for model–observation and multi-model metrics
(type: method; severity: must)
#### Key points
- Regridding model output to a spherical grid "generally involves a land–sea
  mask distinct from the model native mask". [OMIP-phys A3] After remapping,
  each source covers its own set of cells, and coastal cells differ most.
- The GLODAPv2 mapped climatology is a global 1° × 1° product on 33 standard
  depth surfaces, mapped from the GLODAPv2 bottle data. [Lauvset et al. 2016]
  It shares the horizontal geometry of the WOA 1° grid, but not WOA's data
  coverage or its land-sea mask.
#### Checked in the local files
- GLODAPv2.2016b surface `TCO2` has 41320 valid cells on its 1° grid, and
  WOA23 `landsea_01` calls 1459 of them land: 442 north of 60°N, 179 south of
  60°S, the rest coastal cells at all latitudes. `build_comparison_mask` on the
  GLODAP field keeps 39861 cells with `ocean_only=True` and all 41320 with
  `ocean_only=False` (checked 2026-10-20).
#### Implications for this project
- A model is comparable with an observation, or with another model, only where
  both have data, and several models can be ranked against each other on a
  cell-by-cell score (a bias, an RMSE or the pattern correlation of one field)
  only where all of them do. Left to itself each pair covers a different set of
  cells, so such a score from one pair describes a different piece of ocean
  from the next, and a ranking between them means nothing. Put the fields on
  one grid, build one mask over all of them with `build_comparison_mask`, and
  apply it with `.where(mask)` to every field before any metric is taken.
- Where the shared mask does not apply: a pattern that is itself the result of
  an analysis on each source's own domain, such as the regression pattern of a
  variability mode (see `eof-modes-and-pdo-index`). Each mode was computed on
  its own source's cells, so masking the patterns afterwards to the other
  sources' coverage does not make them comparable. Correlate each with the
  reference pattern over the cells the two share (`analyze_correlation` leaves
  out the cells either one lacks), and compare the models on those
  correlations. Nor does a mask built on one level carry to another: coverage
  shrinks with depth, so a statistic at several depths takes its cells from the
  fields at each depth, not from the surface mask.
- The numbers of one cell-by-cell comparison come from one sample. Report the
  means that `compute_difference_metrics` returns over the shared sample
  (`mean_data`, `mean_reference`) together with the bias, not means computed
  separately over different cells. A global or regional mean of each source
  over its own ocean, with its own weights, is a different quantity (see
  `omip-native-grid-vs-regridded`): when the difference of such means is asked
  for, take each mean on its own grid and say that the difference is not the
  bias on the shared mask.
- `ocean_only=True`, the default of `build_comparison_mask`, is right only when
  WOA23 is part of the comparison. The check is made on the grid, so a
  GLODAP-against-models comparison on the shared 1° geometry silently inherits
  WOA23's coastline and drops cells where GLODAP has data and WOA23 says land.
  Those are among the hardest cells for a model to match, so dropping them
  flatters every model. Pass `ocean_only=False` when the reference is not WOA23.
- Report the cell counts the mask records alongside the metrics. A mask much
  smaller than the individual fields means the products disagree about where
  the ocean is, not that the models are poor.
#### Common mistakes
- Scoring each model on its own pairwise mask in a cell-by-cell comparison of
  one field, and then ranking the models on those scores.
- Applying the WOA23 land-sea mask to a comparison against GLODAP.

### [cmip6-tos-bulk-top-cell] CMIP6 tos is the bulk temperature of the top model cell, including under sea ice
(type: concept; severity: should)
#### Key points
- `tos` is the "temperature of upper boundary of the liquid ocean, including
  temperatures below sea-ice and floating ice shelves" (CMOR). Under sea ice it
  is the water temperature just below the ice, not the ice-surface temperature.
  [CMOR Omon; OMIP-phys H13]
- Model surface tracers are **bulk values averaged over the top grid cell**,
  which is generally at least a metre thick. They are not skin values. [OMIP-phys H13]
- "In nearly all ocean climate models, the surface temperature is given by the
  prognostic temperature in the upper-most ocean grid cell." [OMIP-phys H13]
- `tos` is surface potential temperature, which equals surface in-situ
  temperature and differs from surface Conservative Temperature. [OMIP-phys H13]
- Top-cell thickness of the three native ocean grids, according to the CMIP6 source
  registry [CVs source_id]:

  | model | ocean component | top grid cell |
  |---|---|---|
  | CESM2 | POP2, 60 levels | 0–10 m |
  | GFDL-ESM4 | GFDL-OM4p5 (MOM6), 75 levels | 0–2 m |
  | MPI-ESM1-2-LR | MPIOM1.63, 40 levels | 0–12 m |

  The CESM2 paper itself evaluates SST as "0‐ to 10‐m average in the ocean"
  against ERSSTv5. [CESM2 §7]
#### Implications for this project
- Using the first level of `thetao` as a substitute for `tos` (needed for
  ssp245, where `tos` is missing; see `configs/datasets.yaml`) reproduces the
  model's own SST definition, because tos is the top-cell prognostic
  temperature. Minor differences are possible where the archived vertical grid
  differs from the native grid. GFDL-ESM4's archived `thetao` has been
  regridded to 35 z levels, the first centred at 2.5 m. Checked on GFDL-ESM4,
  1990–1999: a Niño3.4 index from the top level of `thetao` matches the one
  from `tos` to an RMS of 0.005 °C. A 0–10 m thickness-weighted average
  (two levels) departs by 0.031 °C. `compute_enso_nino_index` and
  `compute_pdo_index` therefore take the top level by default
  (`depth_level=None`), the like-for-like substitute where `tos` is missing.
  A `depth_level` of 10 m instead averages two levels in GFDL-ESM4 but one in
  CESM2 and MPI-ESM1-2-LR, so it is not the same quantity across models.
- A model's first level stands for its surface. A value asked for at 0 m,
  above every model's shallowest level centre, is that shallowest level's value
  (`regrid_vertical` with `fill_surface=True`).
- An ENSO index from a model is its anomaly from its **own** climatology.
  Subtracting an observed climatology puts the model's mean-state bias into
  the index, which is then not an ENSO index (`compute_enso_nino_index` with
  `climatology='woa23'` on model output does exactly that).
- Sea-ice-covered cells carry the under-ice water temperature (near freezing).
  They are not missing. A global-mean SST therefore includes polar values near
  −1.8 °C. Observational products that also report under-ice SST near freezing
  are comparable. If a product masks sea ice, the ocean masks must be made
  consistent before comparing.

### [monthly-means-are-time-averages] CMIP6 monthly fields are time averages of every model time step, so nonlinear diagnostics from them are not the model's own
(type: pitfall; severity: must)
#### Key points
- Each requested period (day, month, year) "is sampled as a time average. Time
  averages include all model time steps over the given period; there is no
  sub-sampling. Products of time-dependent fields are time-averaged as a
  product". [OMIP-phys A1]
- `mlotstmax`/`mlotstmin` are the extremes over snapshots within the month.
  Adjacent points "need not have extrema registered at the same time step".
  [OMIP-phys A1, H24.1]
- Squared fields (e.g. `tossq`, `mlotstsq`) are accumulated online so that
  variance can be computed. Squaring a monthly mean does not give them.
  [OMIP-phys Table H1 caption, H25]
- MLD is recommended online "in order to avoid aliasing". [OMIP-phys H24.2]
- For extensive quantities, "experience at GFDL with Boussinesq z* models
  suggests that errors are minor when working offline using time averaged
  thickness and time averaged tracer concentration". Errors are "generally
  unacceptable" for isopycnal models. [OMIP-phys F3]
#### Implications for this project
- f(monthly mean) ≠ monthly mean of f for any nonlinear f. Examples are density
  from T and S, MLD by a threshold crossing, depth of a maximum gradient, a
  threshold exceedance (hypoxia) or a ratio of two tracers. The order of
  operations therefore changes the answer and must be chosen on purpose:
  - **diagnostic first, then average** reproduces the mean of the diagnostic
    and matches how archived online diagnostics are defined;
  - **average first, then diagnostic** gives the diagnostic of the mean state.
    This is the like-for-like choice when the reference exists only as a
    climatology.
  State which order was used and why.
- Monthly variance within a month cannot be recovered from monthly means. Only
  interannual or inter-month variance can.
- Averaging monthly fields over a period needs day weights in the model's own
  calendar. `temporal_aggregate` does this, and equal weights do not: calendar
  months are 28 to 31 days long, so an equal-weight average of monthly means is
  not a time average. `compute_basic_stat(op='mean', dim='time')` gives every
  month the same weight. `temporal_aggregate` is the only tool that weights by
  month length and so the only correct way to average monthly data over a
  period that spans different calendar months; its `freq='mean'` gives the
  mean state of a period, the field a bias or an RMSE is measured on. Day
  weights do not enter a monthly climatology, each calendar month averaged
  across the years of a baseline (what an anomaly or a climate index is taken
  against): that is the plain mean of that month's values over the years, as
  climate normals are defined, and `compute_anomaly_vs_own_climatology` and the
  index tools build it so.
#### Common mistakes
- Calling an MLD from climatological T/S profiles "the model's climatological
  MLD". It is a different quantity from the time mean of the monthly MLDs.
- Reporting a variance of monthly means as if it were sub-monthly variability.

### [omip-native-grid-vs-regridded] When to work on the native grid and when regridding is acceptable
(type: method; severity: should)
#### Key points
- Two aims conflict: spherical-grid output is easy to map and difference,
  while native-grid output preserves conservation for budgets. "regridding …
  generally involves a land–sea mask distinct from the model native mask. When
  changing the land–sea mask it is difficult to ensure conservation even when
  using 'conservative' regridding methods." [OMIP-phys A3]
- The loss of conservation "may be of minor consequence for many metric-based
  analysis purposes. However, it sacrifices the needs of budget analyses."
  Regridding "merely for visualization purposes and/or to produce difference
  maps" can ignore non-conservation. [OMIP-phys A3, A3.2]
- Structured-grid models archive on their native grid. The standard spherical
  targets are the 1° or ¼° grids of the World Ocean Atlas. [OMIP-phys A3.3]
- In the vertical, models on z, z\*, p or p\* need no regridding. Coarsening to
  the 33 WOA standard levels is allowed, but "care should be given to models
  that do not share the same 5500 m depth as the World Ocean Atlas".
  [OMIP-phys A4, C2]
- For transports across sections (overturning, passages, heat transport) on
  non-spherical grids, compute along a native-grid "zig-zag" path that
  approximates the section rather than regridding fluxes. [OMIP-phys C4]
#### Implications for this project
- A good pattern for model-versus-observation work: compute every **number**
  (global and basin means, inventories, integrals) on each source's native grid
  with its own weights. Regrid to the WOA 1° grid only for **maps, pattern
  correlation and RMSE on a common mask**. Say which numbers come from which
  grid. The exception is an analysis whose result depends on the grid and is
  compared between sources, such as an EOF mode with its explained variance
  and its regression pattern (see `eof-modes-and-pdo-index`): put every
  source, the observations included, on one common grid before the analysis,
  and take its numbers there.
- After regridding a field for a cell-by-cell comparison, build one shared
  valid-data mask for all sources before computing pattern statistics. Coastal
  cells otherwise differ between the sources (see `common-comparison-mask`,
  which also says when a pair's own shared cells are the sample instead, as
  for the patterns of a variability mode).
- Regrid only for the cell-by-cell comparison. An average, a trend or an
  integral belongs on the field's own grid with its own cell areas, because
  regridding replaces those with the target's.
- Method in `regrid_horizontal`: 'conservative' is the right choice going to a
  coarser grid and before any area-weighted step; 'bilinear' is the fallback
  when a model's corner mesh is unusable; 'nearest_s2d' is for masks and other
  categorical fields. A regridded RMSE or pattern correlation depends on the
  method, so state the method whenever one is quoted.
- Vertical interpolation onto another level set changes layer-integrated
  quantities. Integrate thickness-weighted quantities (inventories, OMZ
  thickness) on the original levels.
- Values at fixed depths (a bias "at 100, 500 and 1000 m") need each product
  interpolated to those depths (`regrid_vertical`), not the nearest level
  selected: vertical grids differ between products and rarely line up, so each
  product would answer at a different depth, and the products differ most
  where the gradients are steepest. This interpolation is for comparison, not
  for budgets.
- Diagnostics located at a vertical extremum, such as a nutricline (the depth of
  the maximum vertical gradient), are quantised by the vertical grid. Where the
  profile is smooth, in the subtropical gyres especially, the maximum can jump
  between levels, so read such a map together with the gradient itself.
#### Common mistakes
- Computing a global inventory after regridding to 1° and comparing it with
  the native-grid value as if they should agree.
- Regridding velocity components as scalars (see
  `velocity-components-are-grid-relative`).

### [serial-correlation-and-significance] Autocorrelated ocean data: significance of trends and correlations, and validation of fitted relations
(type: method; severity: should)
#### Key points
- Serially correlated observations carry fewer independent samples than they
  have time steps. For red noise with lag-one autocorrelation r, the effective
  sample size for the sample mean (order 1) is T(1 − r)/(1 + r). For the
  correlation between two series with lag-one autocorrelations r_X and r_Y it
  is T(1 − r_X r_Y)/(1 + r_X r_Y) (Eq. 31). [Bretherton et al. 1999, §5]
- In practice the autocorrelation has to be estimated from the one series at
  hand, so the effective sample size is itself uncertain, the more so when the
  series has substantial power at periods near its length. [Bretherton et al.
  1999, §5]
#### Implications for this project
- A monthly ocean series is strongly autocorrelated, so its number of steps
  overstates the independent information by a large factor. Uncorrected, a test
  uses far too many degrees of freedom: weak correlations come out highly
  significant, and a monthly trend map is significant almost everywhere, which
  says more about the persistence of the ocean than about any trend.
  `fit_1D_trend`, `fit_trend_map` and `analyze_correlation` apply an effective
  sample size along time by default and return the unadjusted values alongside;
  quote the corrected ones.
- For the question "is this quantity changing, and how fast", fit a straight
  line explicitly: `method='linear'` (the standard choice) or
  `method='theil_sen'` (the same question, resistant to outliers and non-normal
  residuals). Automatic model selection answers "which curve describes this
  series", not "what is the trend": a seasonal series will legitimately select
  the sinusoid. Quote the slope with its confidence interval, and call it
  significant only if the interval excludes zero.
- A trend map is a straight-line fit at every cell, so every cell is estimated
  the same way and read as a rate. Over whole years the raw field can be
  fitted, but its seasonal cycle inflates the lag-1 autocorrelation and makes
  the test conservative; removing the seasonal cycle first gives a sharper test
  and the same trend. Draw the slope everywhere and stipple the significant
  cells, rather than blanking the insignificant ones: a coherent pattern that
  misses significance is still worth seeing. A low R² with a significant slope
  is normal for a noisy field, a real drift buried in variability.
- Correlations along time: when both series contain a long-term trend, detrend
  them first (`detrend=True`), because two independently rising series
  correlate through their trends alone. That is for asking whether two
  different quantities are related. When two estimates of the same quantity are
  compared, such as an index rebuilt from a gridded field against the
  published index, or one series against its counterpart in another product,
  correlate them as they are: their shared trend is part of the agreement being
  checked, not a spurious link. When the effective sample size is much
  smaller than the number of pairs, the correlation rests on that many
  independent observations; a correlation from a handful of points is unstable.
- Across space no such correction exists for a pattern correlation: read its
  coefficient, not its p-value.
- Fitted relations between fields (`fit_multivariate_relation`): neighbouring
  ocean cells are near-duplicates, so a random train/test split scores a model
  against its own neighbours and reports a skill it does not have. Hold out
  whole spatial blocks larger than the field's correlation length scale; a
  random split suits only data with no spatial structure. Ocean tracers are
  often strongly collinear, which makes individual coefficients unreliable even
  when the fit is good; the variance inflation factors show when that happens.
#### Common mistakes
- Quoting a trend's p-value or interval from the naive sample size of a monthly
  series.
- Letting automatic model selection answer a trend question.

### [cmip6-experiments-historical-ssp245] What the CMIP6 historical and ssp245 experiments are
(type: dataset; severity: should)
#### Key points
| experiment | description | years | parent | activity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| historical | "all-forcing simulation of the recent past" | 1850–2014 (min 165 yr) | piControl (or past1000/past2k) | CMIP |
| ssp245 | "Future scenario with medium radiative forcing by the end of century. Following approximately RCP4.5 global forcing pathway but with new forcing based on SSP2. **Concentration-driven**" | 2015–2100 (min 86 yr) | historical | ScenarioMIP |
| piControl | "pre-industrial control" | ≥ 500 yr | piControl-spinup | CMIP |
| esm-hist | historical with **CO2 emission-driven**, atmospheric CO2 calculated | 1850–2014 | esm-piControl | CMIP |

[CVs experiment_id]

- The coupled historical run differs from the forced ocean-only OMIP runs
  (omip1) "only" in initialisation and forcing. omip1 is initialised from
  observations and forced by reanalysis, while historical is coupled after a
  spin-up. [OMIP-BGC §2]
#### Implications for this project
- Both local experiments are **concentration-driven** (not esm-hist). Atmospheric
  CO2 is prescribed, so ocean carbon uptake differences between models do not
  feed back on atmospheric CO2.
- Coupled runs have their own internal variability. A given model year (e.g.
  the 1997 El Niño year) is **not** expected to match the observed year.
  Compare statistics (means, variances, spectra, trends over long windows),
  not individual years or event timing.
- A single member per model (see `historical-ssp245-member-continuity`)
  cannot separate forced change from internal variability. Short-window trends
  should carry that caveat.

===== USER =====
User question: Assess the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO) simulated by three CMIP6 models against ERSSTv5 observations, over 1950-01 to 2014-12.

Data to use:
- CMIP6 historical `tos` from CESM2, GFDL-ESM4 and MPI-ESM1-2-LR, monthly, 1950-01 to 2014-12.
- ERSSTv5 monthly SST over the same window.
- The published PDO index (product `pdo` in the index registry).

The PDO is the leading EOF of monthly North Pacific SST anomalies over 20N-70N, 110E-100W, relative to a 1950-2014 monthly climatology.

Expected numerical results, for each of the four sources over 1950-2014, with any regridding done by bilinear interpolation onto the 1-degree grid:
- The percentage of variance explained by the leading mode, to 1 decimal place.
- The amplitude of the mode, in degrees Celsius per standard deviation, to 3 decimal places.
- The lag-1-year (12-month) autocorrelation of the monthly standardized index, to 3 decimal places.
- The Pearson correlation of the ERSSTv5-derived index with the published PDO series, to 3 decimal places, and what that correlation does and does not demonstrate.
- For each model, the spatial Pearson correlation between its regression pattern and the ERSSTv5 regression pattern, to 3 decimal places.
- The observed PDO phase-transition years over 1950-2014, defined as the years in which the 9-year (108-month) centred running mean of the ERSSTv5 index changes sign (a crossing less than 3 years after the last counted transition is merged into it, keeping the earlier year), and which of them correspond to documented Pacific climate shifts.

<knowledge_notes>
[eof-modes-and-pdo-index]
- Anomalies are SST minus the climatological annual cycle and minus the global-mean SST anomaly at each grid point; the EOF is taken on area-weighted data, weights `sqrt(cos(latitude))`; fix the sign so positive index = negative SST anomalies in the central and western North Pacific, and standardise the index (zero mean, unit variance).
- The EOF is dimensionless: report the mode's amplitude from the regression pattern (anomalies regressed on the standardised index, °C per standard deviation), and its area-weighted RMS in °C; compare explained variance only between fields on the same grid over the same period.
[anomaly-baselines-and-climate-indices]
- Take each source's anomaly against its **own** 1950-2014 monthly climatology (models and ERSSTv5 alike) so mean-state bias cancels, and state the baseline in the answer; an offset between series built on different baselines is not an error.
- A monthly index is matched against the climatology of its own calendar month, which removes the seasonal cycle; do not mix the DJF and JFM/AMJ/JAS/OND season conventions in one comparison.
[common-comparison-mask]
- Masking a regression pattern to another source's coverage after the EOF does not make the patterns comparable: correlate each model's pattern with the ERSSTv5 pattern over the cells the two share (`analyze_correlation` leaves out cells either lacks, `weighting='area'`), and rank the models on those correlations.
- Report the shared-cell counts alongside the pattern correlations; a mask much smaller than the individual fields means the products disagree about where the ocean is, not that the models are poor.
[cmip6-tos-bulk-top-cell]
- `tos` is the bulk top-cell temperature including under sea ice (near-freezing values, not missing); `compute_pdo_index` takes the top level by default (`depth_level=None`) — a `depth_level` of 10 m is not the same quantity across these models.
- Never give a model index an observed climatology (`climatology='woa23'`): that puts the model's mean-state bias into the index.
[monthly-means-are-time-averages]
- The 1950-2014 monthly climatology is the plain mean of each calendar month across the years, as climate normals are defined; day weights do not enter it.
[omip-native-grid-vs-regridded]
- An EOF mode with its explained variance and regression pattern depends on the grid: put all four sources, observations included, on the one 1° grid before the analysis and take every reported number there, with `regrid_horizontal` method `'bilinear'` as the question specifies — state the method.
[serial-correlation-and-significance]
- Correlate the ERSSTv5-derived index with the published PDO as it is (no `detrend`): the shared trend is part of the agreement being checked; quote the effective-sample-size-corrected significance that `analyze_correlation` returns by default.
- No correction exists for a pattern correlation across space: read its coefficient, not its p-value; the 12-month lag autocorrelation is the persistence figure to report as is.
[cmip6-experiments-historical-ssp245]
- Historical runs carry their own internal variability with a single member, so individual years and event timing are not comparable: compare statistics (means, variances, trends, explained variance, amplitude, persistence), and define transition years only for the ERSSTv5 index.
